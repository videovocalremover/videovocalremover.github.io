Audio Formats for Backing Tracks: WAV, FLAC, MP3 or M4A?

Anyone who builds a collection of karaoke or backing tracks ends up with a mix of file types. The right format depends on what you are doing with the file at that moment. A good rule: keep quality while you work, and only compress at the very end.

The formats in one table

FormatTypeTypical size, 4-minute songBest for
WAVUncompressedabout 40 MBEditing, separation, key changes
FLACLossless compressedabout 20-25 MBArchiving your masters
MP3 (320 kbps)Lossyabout 9-10 MBPlaying on almost any device
M4A / AAC (256 kbps)Lossyabout 8 MBApple devices, phones
OGG VorbisLossyabout 7-9 MBGames, some web players

Stage by stage

  • Editing and separating: use WAV. Every tool reads it, and there is no decode step that could add artefacts before you remove a vocal or change the key.
  • Storing: FLAC keeps every bit of a WAV at roughly half the size, and it carries tags (artist, title, key) properly.
  • Playing at a venue or party: a 320 kbps MP3 is indistinguishable from WAV for most listeners on a PA system, and every laptop, phone and karaoke player accepts it.
  • Sending to someone: MP3 or M4A, because email and chat apps often limit attachment size.

The one mistake to avoid

Do not convert a lossy file to another lossy file again and again, for example MP3 to M4A to MP3. Each round throws away a bit more detail and adds swirly artefacts, especially on cymbals and reverb tails. Go back to the WAV or FLAC master and encode once.

What about a separated track that started as MP3?

If the only copy of a song you have is an MP3, that is fine. Decode it to WAV for the separation or editing step, save the result as WAV, and make a single MP3 copy at the end for playback.
