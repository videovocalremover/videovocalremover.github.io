Covers, Karaoke and Stems: A Plain-English Guide to Using Separated Tracks

Separating the vocal from a song takes a minute now. Knowing what you are allowed to do with the result takes longer. This page sums up the common situations in plain language so musicians, teachers and video makers can make sensible choices.

This is general information, not legal advice. Copyright rules differ by country and change over time. If money or a commercial release is involved, check with a music rights professional.

Two separate rights in every song

A commercial track usually involves two different copyrights:

  • The composition: the melody and lyrics, owned by the songwriters and their publishers.
  • The sound recording: the actual audio, owned by the label or artist who paid for it.

When you separate stems, you are still using the sound recording. A separated instrumental is a modified copy of the original recording, not a new work.

Common situations

What you want to doTypical position
Practise singing or playing along at homeGenerally low risk: private, non-commercial use of music you have access to.
Use a backing track for a live gigThe venue normally holds a public performance licence for the composition. Using a commercial recording as the backing is a grey area; many performers use licensed karaoke tracks instead.
Upload a cover video to YouTube with the separated instrumentalLikely to be claimed by Content ID because the recording is recognised. Revenue usually goes to the rights owner, or the video may be blocked in some regions.
Teach a class using isolated vocalsClassroom and educational exceptions exist in many countries but are narrow. Playing in class is safer than redistributing files.
Release a remix or use a vocal as a sampleNeeds permission (clearance) from the recording owner and the publisher. Separation does not change this.
Separate your own recordings or royalty-free musicNo issue: you own or have licensed the recording.

Safer ways to get the result you want

  • Re-record the instrumental. If you play or program the backing yourself, you only need to deal with the composition, which is much easier to license for covers.
  • Use licensed karaoke or backing-track libraries for public performances and uploads.
  • Keep separated files private. Do not share stems from commercial songs publicly, even if you made them yourself.
  • Work with your own material. Stem separation is excellent for fixing your own old demos where the multitrack files were lost.

Where separation tools fit

Tools that remove vocals from a song online are most useful for practice, transcription, teaching, rehearsal and working with recordings you own. For anything public or commercial, treat the separated track as what it legally is: part of someone else's recording.

Quick checklist before you publish

  1. Is the recording mine or licensed for this use?
  2. If it is a cover, have I cleared or licensed the composition where required?
  3. Will a platform's content matching system recognise the original audio?
  4. Am I sharing stems or just a finished performance?
  5. Would I be comfortable if the rights owner saw this?
